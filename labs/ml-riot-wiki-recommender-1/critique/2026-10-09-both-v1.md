## Change or fix

### C1. P1. No baseline

- **Evidence:** the results table has only the model (lines 71-81).
- **Effect:** TF-IDF cosine with no training may give the same P@5. Then
  the 25.6M-parameter MLP, the GPU, and the weekly retrain add cost and
  no value. The model cannot pass the complexity gate.
- **Alternative:** score these on the same test set and metric:
  - TF-IDF or BM25 cosine with no training.
  - Item-item co-click counts.
  - The links that are already on page A.
  - Global popularity (the floor).
  - An off-the-shelf sentence embedding, no fine-tuning (a cheap version
    of the "BERT" next step at line 96).
  - Keep the MLP only if it beats the best one by more than the noise.

### C2. P1. The label learns the existing link graph

- **Evidence:** "a user reads article A, then clicks through to article
  B. That is a positive signal" (lines 26-27).
- **Effect:** most click-throughs probably follow links that are already
  on page A. The model then recommends links that the user can already
  see. A quick bounce also counts as a positive. See Q2 for the full
  explanation.
- **Alternative:**
  - Tag each click: on-page link, or other navigation (search then open,
    same-session co-read).
  - Down-weight link clicks, or use them only as a baseline.
  - Add a dwell-time threshold, so that a positive means "read".
  - Compare with the links-on-page baseline.
- **Condition:** if link clicks are under about 30% of positives (Q2),
  this item drops to P2.

### C3. P1. A random split leaks, and there is no validation set

- **Evidence:** "Split: 80/20 random" (line 36). "Epochs: 50" (line 68),
  with no validation set.
- **Effect:**
  - The same pairs (A to B, B to A, repeat clicks) and the same articles
    are in train and test.
  - Future clicks train the model that is tested on past clicks.
  - The test 0.88 is too high. It does not show weekly-retrain behavior
    (lines 89-90).
  - If the test set chose the epochs or lr, it is optimistic twice.
- **Alternative:**
  - Time split: train months 1-10, validate month 11, test month 12.
  - Remove duplicate pairs before the split.
  - Early stopping and tuning on validation only.
  - A separate slice for articles that are new in the test period.

### C4. P1. The metrics measure the wrong task

- **Evidence:** AUC and accuracy on pairs (lines 75-76). "Precision@5 is
  lower, but recommendations are subjective so this is expected" (lines
  79-81).
- **Effect:**
  - Pair AUC uses 1 positive against 4 easy random negatives. The
    product ranks 42K articles and shows 5.
  - At 1:4, a constant "not clicked" gets accuracy 0.80. So 0.85 is only
    +0.05 over a constant.
  - The author dismisses the one metric that matches the product.
  - With 1 true item per query, P@5 can be at most 0.20. So 0.31 needs
    an explanation (Q1).
- **Alternative:**
  - Primary: recall@5 and nDCG@5 over the full catalog, with a bootstrap
    CI over query articles. Ground truth: all B clicked from A in the
    test period.
  - Add catalog coverage (share of articles that ever reach a top 5).
  - Keep AUC as a training diagnostic only. Drop accuracy.

### C5. P2. Weak loss and easy negatives

- **Evidence:** BCE on cosine; "4 random articles are sampled as
  negatives" (lines 51-61).
- **Effect:** the model learns topic separation, not the top-5 order
  among similar pages. This explains part of the "high AUC, low P@5"
  pattern. BCE on a raw cosine also saturates (Q4, T1).
- **Alternative:**
  - In-batch sampled softmax (InfoNCE) with a learned temperature and a
    logQ popularity correction. Batch 1024 gives 1023 negatives free.
  - Hard negatives: TF-IDF neighbors of A that were not clicked. Later,
    shown-but-not-clicked items from impression logs.

## Missing

### M1. P1. Goal and online success metric

- **Why:** the background names 2 pains: "cannot find what they need"
  and onboarding search time (lines 12-13). The report has no target. So
  nobody can say if the launch worked, and the offline metric stands in
  for nothing.
- **Standard method:** one primary online metric with a target, for
  example panel CTR with dwell over 30 s, or fewer searches after an
  article view. A new-hire slice ("time to first useful doc" in week 1).
  Write the chain: goal, then recall@5 offline, then the online metric.

### M2. P1. Serving-time filters

- **Why:**
  - Nearest neighbors do not check access. Restricted post-mortems,
    security runbooks, and unreleased specs can leak by title. This is a
    launch blocker.
  - A is its own nearest neighbor (cosine 1.0) and takes 1 of 5 slots.
  - Near-duplicate page versions fill the panel.
  - Archived pages from 3 years ago (line 24) rank well on text.
- **Standard method:** retrieve the top 50. Then filter: the viewer's
  ACL, the query item, near duplicates, archived or deprecated pages.
  Then keep 5.

### M3. P2. Online test, rollout, and fallback

- **Why:** "Deploy behind an internal API" (line 94) has no test, ramp,
  or kill switch. Offline click metrics often do not predict online
  value. 3,400 users give low A/B power.
- **Standard method:** interleaving against the TF-IDF baseline (it
  needs much less traffic), or a user-level A/B test with a stated MDE
  and a power check. A feature-flag kill switch. TF-IDF cosine as the
  fallback. The "was this helpful" button (line 95) as a guardrail. Log
  impressions and positions from day 1.

### M4. P2. Promotion gate for the weekly retrain

- **Why:** "Every week, retrain ... and recompute all embeddings" (lines
  89-90) has no check. A bad week ships with no warning. A TF-IDF
  vocabulary refit changes the embedding space. A partial index update
  mixes 2 incompatible spaces.
- **Standard method:** gate each new model on time-split recall@5 (no
  worse than the current model and the baseline). Version the model and
  its index together. Swap the index atomically. Keep the previous pair
  for rollback.

### M5. P2. Slices and a personalization check (M5 and M6 merged)

- **Why:** a global metric hides the long tail and the new hires that the
  goal names (line 13). The model input is only article A (line 18), so
  every user sees the same 5 articles. Different users may need
  different articles for the same A (see Q6).
- **Standard method:**
  - Report recall@5 by slice. Item side: zero-click articles, articles
    new in the test period, doc type. Query side: users in their first
    90 days vs others, and team vs team.
  - Compare the clicked B for the same A between segments. If the
    segments differ, personalization has value.
  - If so, pass the complexity gate with a simple version first: keep
    the item-to-item top 50, then re-rank with tenure, team, and home
    wiki space. Compare on the new-hire slice. No user embeddings: 3,400
    users and sparse clicks do not support them.

## Questions

Each question has: the expected answer, why it matters, and what the
answer changes.

### Q1. How did you compute P@5?

Full question: how did you compute P@5? Over which candidates? How many
true items per query?

- **Expected:** not clear, or over a small candidate set. With 1 true
  item, the maximum is 0.20. So 0.31 implies several true items or a
  small candidate set.
- **Why it matters:** P@5 needs a set of "relevant" articles for each A.
  That set is the evaluation label. So this question opens the label
  discussion.

```
 P@5 = (relevant items in top 5) / 5
            |
            '--> what is "relevant" for A?   <-- the label question
                   |-- which signal?   (click, dwell, co-read, rating)
                   |-- how many per A? (1 click or all test-period clicks)
                   '-- graded?         (click count gives nDCG gain)
```

Follow-ups, in order:

1. **What counts as relevant for A?** One test click, all test-period
   clicks from A, or clicks above a dwell time?
   - Expected: all A to B clicks in the test rows, no dwell filter.
2. **Is the label complete?** An article that nobody clicked is not
   always irrelevant. Users can click only what they see.
   - Expected: no. So P@5 underestimates quality, and recall@5 is the
     fairer metric.
3. **Is the training label the same as the evaluation label?** If
   training uses clicks and the goal is "found a useful doc", they can
   differ.
   - Expected: both are clicks.
4. **"Recommendations are subjective": did you measure that?** A
   subjective target is a reason to measure it, not to ignore the metric.
   - Standard method: a golden set. For example, 3 engineers rate the top
     5 for 100 query articles. Measure rater agreement and P@5 on the
     ratings.
   - Expected: no golden set exists.

### Q2. What share of positives are on-page link clicks?

**What it means.** Page A has hyperlinks that its author wrote. A user on
A can reach B in 2 ways:

```
 (a) on-page link:  A --[link on A]--> B      the author already connected A and B
 (b) other path:    A --> search "..." --> B  the reader needed B, and A had no link
                    A --> (same session) --> B
```

The report counts all click-throughs as positives. On a wiki, most are
probably type (a). This is a guess, so it is a question.

- **Expected:** most of them, likely 70% or more.
- **Why it matters:**
  - **The model learns what users can already see.** If most positives
    are type (a), the model copies the link graph. The panel then shows
    links that are already on the page. That adds almost no value.
  - **The offline score looks good for the wrong reason.** Test
    positives are also mostly link clicks. A "links on page" baseline can
    score as high with no ML.
  - **Articles with no inbound links get no positives.** Orphan pages are
    the hardest to find, and they are the main target of the product.
  - **Type (b) is the valuable signal.** It shows where the link graph
    fails. That is the gap the recommender must fill.
- **What the answer changes:**
  - High share (70% or more): change the label and weight type (b) up.
    Remove links that are already on A from the 5 slots, or report
    results with and without them. Add the links-on-page baseline.
  - Low share: keep the label. C2 drops to P2.

### Q3. How many articles have zero clicks?

Full question: how many of the 42K articles have zero clicks? How many
users make most of the events?

- **Expected:** many articles (3 years of articles, 12 months of clicks).
  A few heavy users make most of the 1.2M events (about 350 per user).
- **Why it matters:** content retrieval must carry the long tail. A few
  teams can define "related" for everyone.
- **What the answer changes:** many zero-click articles: add the
  zero-click slice (M5). Heavy users: cap or weight events per user.

### Q4. How did you choose 50 epochs and lr=0.001, and on which data?

- **Expected:** defaults or the test set. The 0.97 vs 0.88 gap suggests
  overfit at 50 epochs.
- **Why it matters:** with no validation set, the test score is
  optimistic (C3).

Training-detail follow-ups, sorted by how much each can change the
result (T1 and T2 are the top picks for the interview: concrete, quick to
sketch, and each explains part of "high AUC, low P@5"):

1. **T1. How do you turn cosine into a probability for BCE? Is there a
   scale or temperature?**
   - Expected: no scale; sigmoid(cosine) directly.
   - Why: cosine is in [-1, 1], so the sigmoid output is only in
     [0.27, 0.73]. The loss cannot reach 0, and gradients are weak. A
     learned temperature fixes this.
2. **T2. Can a sampled negative be a real positive? Uniform or
   popularity sampling?**
   - Expected: uniform, no check.
   - Why: false negatives teach wrong pairs. Uniform negatives are easy,
     so AUC is high while P@5 stays low.
3. **T3. Do you deduplicate repeat A to B pairs? Do you cap events per
   user?**
   - Expected: no dedup, no cap.
   - Why: about 350 events per user lets a few teams define "related".
     Duplicates across the random split leak.
4. **T4. Did you use dropout, weight decay, or a smaller first layer?**
   - Expected: none.
   - Why: the first layer has 25.6M weights (50K x 512) for about 1M
     positives. The train/test gap fits overfitting.
5. **T5. Which text goes into TF-IDF, and on which data is it fit? Is it
   refit each week?**
   - Expected: full body, refit each week.
   - Why: a weekly refit changes the input space. Embeddings from 2
     weeks cannot be mixed in one index (M4).
6. **T6. Do you retrain from scratch or warm-start? Are the lists stable
   week to week?**
   - Expected: from scratch, stability not measured.
   - Why: users lose trust if "related" lists jump each week. Measure
     top-5 overlap week over week.
7. **T7. Do you have train and validation curves? Which seeds? What is
   the run-to-run variance?**
   - Expected: train loss only, one seed.
   - Why: without variance, a 0.01 difference between versions is noise.
8. **T8. Is a V100 needed? What does one weekly retrain cost?**
   - Expected: not measured.
   - Why: this is the complexity gate. TF-IDF cosine needs no training
     and no GPU.

### Q5. Who is the main user, and what outcome shows success?

- **Expected:** new hires and engineers who search. Success is less time
  to find a doc, not pair AUC.
- **Why it matters:** it sets M1 (the goal) and the slices in M5.

### Q6. "Subjective" in which sense?

Full question: "subjective" in which sense: do raters disagree, or do
users need different articles for the same A?

- **Expected:** the author did not separate the 2 meanings.

```
 "recommendations are subjective"
        |
        |-- (a) raters disagree about relevance       --> label noise
        |       same user type, same A, different opinions
        |       fix: measure agreement, golden set
        |
        '-- (b) different users need different B's    --> personalization
                for the same A
                fix: add user context to the model
```

- **(a) Label noise.** A golden set with 2-3 raters per list measures
  it. Good agreement (for example kappa > 0.6): the low P@5 is a model
  problem. Poor agreement: offline P@5 has a ceiling, and the online
  metric matters more.
- **(b) Personalization (p13n).** The model input is only A (line 18),
  so every user sees the same 5 articles. Example for A = "Deploy service
  X":
  - New hire, week 1: onboarding guide, glossary, setup runbook.
  - Senior engineer on team X: recent post-mortem, design spec v2.
  - Engineer from another team: API doc, owner contact page.
  The goal names new hires (line 13), so (b) touches the core goal.
- **How to tell (a) from (b) with data:** for the same A, compare the
  clicked B between segments (new hires vs others, team vs team).
  Different B: (b). Same B but raters disagree: (a).
- **What the answer changes:** (a) needs a golden set. (b) needs the
  re-rank in M5.

## Appendix

Findings that the Quick limits did not present, sorted by importance:

- P2: Overfit: train AUC 0.97 vs test 0.88, fixed 50 epochs (line 75).
  Add early stopping and regularization (see T4).
- P2: A vector DB is extra infrastructure for 42K x 64-d. A weekly
  precomputed top-50 table or in-memory brute force gives the same
  result (line 86).
- P2: "Explore BERT embeddings" (line 96) fails the complexity gate
  before a baseline exists. First try an untrained sentence embedding.
- P2: No requirements: latency, QPS, availability, cost.
- P2: The windows differ: articles cover 3 years, clicks 12 months
  (lines 24, 31). Old articles have no label signal, and some are stale.
- P2: Shared weights plus cosine make A to B equal to B to A. The model
  loses reading order (onboarding sequences). Directional co-click adds
  it.
- P2: No catalog coverage or popularity-concentration check.
- P3: The report does not say if the TF-IDF vocabulary is fit only on
  train articles.
- P3: Stubs are removed from training (line 25), but stubs can still be
  query pages. The fallback is not defined.
- P3: No seed, no multi-seed variance; fixed lr, no schedule.
- P3: New articles can be embedded at publish time (content-only tower).
  The plan does not say so.
- P3: No qualitative review of top-5 lists for sample queries.
- P3: No diversity re-rank; the 5 slots can show near duplicates.
- P3: No "why recommended" hint to build trust.
- P3: The feedback button gives sparse signal. Log impressions and
  positions too, for hard negatives and position-bias correction.
